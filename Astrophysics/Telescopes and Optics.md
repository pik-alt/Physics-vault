
**A lens is a curved piece of glass that curves incident rays of light**

**The point that all the incident light rays converge upon after hitting a lens is called the Focus, F, and the distance from the lens to the focus is the Focal Length,f.**

![[Convex and concave lenses.png|342]]

*For a Convex lens, f > 0, while for a Concave lens, f < 0*

The power of a lens, P, is defined as the reciprocal of the focal length:
$P=\frac{1}{f}$


![[object and image distance.png|332]]

*The distance from the lens to the object is u, defined as positive to the left
The distance from the lens to the image is v, defined as positive to the right*

The Magnification of a lens is defined by:
$M=-\frac{v}{u}$


To find where an image will be displayed, you can use the Lens Equation:
$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v}$

All real images images, (where v > 0) are inverted.

## Telescopes

Telescopes use two lenses, an Objective lens and the Eye piece, to magnify very distant objects.
Because the objects are so distant, we can approximate the incoming light rays as basically parallel.

![[refraction magnification.png|537]]

*The eyepiece converts the light rays into near perpendicular beams, which are then extrapolated by the human eye into the final image*

Refracting telescopes are hard to make as the lens needs to be near perfect and ideally as large as possible.

Because the angle of refraction experienced by light depends on it's wavelength, different colours of light can appear to be out of focus, what is called Chromatic Aberration.

This can be solved with mirrors.


Reflecting Telescopes use a mirror to focus distant and parallel light into a point that can then be detected by an eye or camera.
![[types of reflecting telescopes.png|431]]

*Different types of telescope design. As you can see, all of them somewhat block the aperture with either the detector or a secondary mirror*

Reflecting telescopes eliminate chromatic aberration because the angle of reflection does not depend on wavelength.
