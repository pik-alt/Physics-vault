### Image Scale

A distant object with angular size $\theta$ and projected image of size $s$ has an image scale, $p$, of:
$p = \frac{\theta}{s} = \frac{1}{f}$

Units are radians per metre, however often given in arc seconds per mm.


### Collecting Area

Basically the area of the primary mirror. The higher the collecting area the sharper the contrast.

$A=\frac{\pi D^2}{4}$ 
We use diameter, D, for this because telescopes are often given by the size of their diameter, also known as its aperture.


### Focal Ratio

Defined as the ratio of the focal length, f, to the diameter, D.

$F=\frac{f}{D}$

A smaller F means that the final image is brighter (because more light is being collected) but, since the final image is larger, it's less detailed.

### Angular Resolution

This is the ability to be able to discern closely spaced sources. It is the smallest angle that you can resolve.

$\theta = 1.2 \times \frac{\lambda}{D}$

Where $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the light you're looking to resolve and $D$ is the diameter of the telescope.

Lower $\theta$ is better.

The Earth's atmosphere contains pockets of air at different temperatures which act as lenses, refracting the light we see and decreasing our angular resolution, which is why telescopes on Earth suck balls.

$\theta_{seeing} = 1.2 \times \frac{\lambda}{r_{0}}$
where $r_{0} = \approx \text{ size of an air pocket}$

To solve this problem, you can either build the telescope at very high altitude or use adaptive optics.
