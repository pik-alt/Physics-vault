A moving source of electromagnetic waves compresses the waves in the direction of motion, shortening the wavelength.

$\text{ For } v\ll c: \newline v_{\text{doppler}} = c \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_{rest}}$

Where $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_{\text{Observed}} - \lambda_{\text{Rest}}$


When measuring the doppler shift to find the velocity, you can only find the radial velocity from Earth, not the angular velocity.

We can use doppler shift to detect exoplanets. Large enough exoplanets cause their stars to have a small orbit, which then slightly doppler shift the observed light from that star.

There's a long derivation to get a formula for the mass of an exoplanet based on factors of the star.

$M_{p} \sin i = M_{s}V_{s}\sqrt{ \frac{r_{p}}{GM_{s}} }$

$M_{p} = \text{Mass of exoplanet} \newline M_{s}=\text{Mass of star} \newline V_{s} = \text{ Velocity of star} \newline r_{p} =\text{Orbital radius of exoplanet} \newline i = \text{angle of inclination}$


---

Redshift is the overall velocity of a galaxy (or other object) as observed by us. Most things in the universe are moving away from us due to cosmic expansion.

$z= \frac{\lambda_{\text{Observed}} - \lambda_{\text{Rest}}}{\lambda_{\text{Rest}}}$

If you're working with frequencies instead of wavelengths, swap "Observed" and "Rest".
