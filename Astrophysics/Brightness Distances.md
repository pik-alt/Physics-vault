Luminosity, L, is the power emitted by a star; units $W$
Flux, F, is the power received over a $1m^2$ distance from some distance, d, away; units $Wm^-2$

$F=\frac{L}{4\pi d^2}$


We categorise stars using the magnitude scale, where the brighter a star is, the lower it's apparent magnitude (apparent magnitude being what's detected on Earth).
It is a **Logarithmic Scale** that runs from approx. -1 - 25.

If something is one magnitude fainter than something else, that means the difference in flux is a factor of 2.512.

**If the Flux Ratio between two objects decreases by 100, the magnitude increases by 5**

$\frac{F_{1}}{F_{2}} = 100 \times \frac{m_{2}-m_{1}}{5}$

Which can re-arrange into:

$m_{1} - m_{2} = -2.5 \log \frac{F_{1}}{F_{2}}$
or
$\frac{F_{1}}{F_{2}} = 10^{-0.4 \times (m_{1} - m_{2})}$


We define the star Vega to have a magnitude of 0.


**The Absolute Magnitude, $M$, is the magnitude a star will have at a distance of 10 parsec**

We can use the definitions of flux and magnitude to find a way of converting between apparent and absolute magnitude with some algebra:

$m-M = 5\log (d) - 5$

where $d$ is the distance in parsecs

## Standard Candles

**Cepheids are stars that pulsate at a period of days/months. They follow a very strict periodicity: the longer the period, the more luminous, linearly.**

This means that if we can measure the period $P$, then we can measure the absolute brightness.

**Supernovae of type 1-a have a uniform peak brightness of $M=-19.3$**


To calibrate our distance to cepheids, we can use parallax.
To calibrate our distance to supernovae, we can use nearby cepheids.

This technique is called a **distance ladder.**
